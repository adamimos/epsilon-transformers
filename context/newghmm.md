# The Quantum RRXOR Process

*A quantum generalization of the Random-Random-XOR process, derived from a repeating quantum circuit with persistent qubit memory.*

---

## 1. Motivation: From Classical to Quantum RRXOR

### 1.1 Classical RRXOR

The Random-Random-XOR (RRXOR) process is a classical stochastic process over the binary alphabet $\mathcal{X} = \{0, 1\}$ generated by a 5-state hidden Markov model with states $\mathcal{S} = \{S, 0, 1, T, F\}$. The process operates in a repeating 3-step cycle:

1. **Random bit**: State $S$ emits a random bit $x_1$ with probability $p_1$ (for 0) or $1-p_1$ (for 1), transitioning to state $0$ or $1$ respectively.
2. **Random bit**: The current state ($0$ or $1$) emits another random bit $x_2$ with probability $p_2$/$1-p_2$, transitioning to state $T$ (if $x_1 \oplus x_2 = 1$) or $F$ (if $x_1 \oplus x_2 = 0$).
3. **Deterministic readout**: State $T$ emits $1$ deterministically; state $F$ emits $0$ deterministically. Both return to $S$.

The third token thus reveals the XOR of the first two, creating non-trivial temporal correlations. The belief states—probability distributions over the 5 hidden states induced by observed histories—form a fractal geometry in the 4-simplex.

### 1.2 Leaky RRXOR

The *leaky* version softens the deterministic transitions by mixing with a uniform distribution:

$$T^{(x)}_\varepsilon = (1 - \varepsilon)\, T^{(x)}_{\text{RRXOR}} + \frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{X}| \cdot |\mathcal{S}|}\, \mathbf{J}$$

where $\mathbf{J}$ is the all-ones matrix. This blends each transition with a uniform probability over all (token, next-state) pairs, controlled by the leakage parameter $\varepsilon \in [0, 1]$.

### 1.3 Why go quantum?

The classical RRXOR has a finite HMM—its belief geometry, while fractal, lives in a finite-dimensional simplex. We seek a process that:

- Has the same binary alphabet $|\mathcal{X}| = 2$ (so next-token probabilities are a single scalar $p(0)$),
- Has belief geometry of dimension $\geq 2$ (so the map from beliefs to next-token probabilities is non-invertible),
- Has **no finite HMM** (genuinely requires quantum resources), and
- Arises naturally from a quantum circuit generalizing the RRXOR structure.

The key insight, following Riechers & Crutchfield (2021), is that classical processes like RRXOR can be represented as quantum circuits with intermediate measurements. Generalizing these circuits—by replacing Hadamard gates with parameterized rotations and keeping the memory qubit coherent—yields genuinely quantum processes.

## 2. The Quantum Circuit

### 2.1 Circuit structure

The Quantum RRXOR is defined by a repeating quantum circuit acting on two qubits: a persistent **memory qubit** $|\psi\rangle_{\text{mem}}$ (never measured) and a fresh **ancilla qubit** $|0\rangle_{\text{anc}}$ (measured at each step to produce the classical token).

Each timestep applies:

$$|\psi\rangle_{\text{mem}} \;\xrightarrow{R_y(\varphi)}\; \xrightarrow{\text{CNOT}}\; \xrightarrow{I \otimes R_x(\theta)}\; \xrightarrow{\text{measure anc.}}\; |\psi'\rangle_{\text{mem}}, \; x_t$$

In circuit notation:

```
|ψ⟩_mem ──R_y(φ)──●─────────── |ψ'⟩_mem   (persistent, never measured)
                   │
|0⟩_anc ──────────⊕──R_x(θ)──╱── x_t      (fresh each step, measured)
```

The two parameters control distinct aspects of the process:

- **$\varphi$ (memory rotation)**: $R_y(\varphi)$ rotates the memory qubit *before* it interacts with the ancilla. This mixes the $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ components, creating off-diagonal elements in the Kraus operators. When $\varphi/\pi$ is irrational, the process has no finite HMM.

- **$\theta$ (readout angle)**: $R_x(\theta)$ rotates the ancilla *after* the CNOT but *before* measurement. This controls how informative each token is about the memory state. At $\theta = 0$, the ancilla is always measured as $|0\rangle$ regardless of the memory (completely uninformative). At $\theta = \pi$, the measurement is maximally informative, approaching the classical limit.

### 2.2 Physical interpretation

The circuit implements a quantum version of "observe and update":

1. **Rotate memory** ($R_y(\varphi)$): The memory qubit's state is rotated, evolving it through a continuous manifold of quantum states. This is the quantum analog of the classical state transition.

2. **Entangle** (CNOT): The memory qubit is entangled with a fresh ancilla. The CNOT copies the computational basis information: $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ becomes $\alpha|00\rangle + \beta|11\rangle$.

3. **Soft readout** ($R_x(\theta)$): Instead of directly measuring the entangled ancilla (which would fully collapse the memory), we first rotate the ancilla. This makes the measurement basis non-aligned with the entanglement basis, yielding a *partial* or *soft* readout.

4. **Measure ancilla**: The ancilla is measured in the computational basis, producing token $x_t \in \{0, 1\}$. This collapses the ancilla but only *partially* disturbs the memory qubit—it remains in a coherent superposition, carrying quantum information forward to the next step.

The crucial feature: **the memory qubit is never measured directly**. It maintains quantum coherence across all timesteps, accumulating a history of rotations and partial measurements that creates complex temporal correlations in the classical token stream.

## 3. Kraus Operators

### 3.1 Derivation

The Kraus operators $K_x$ for measurement outcome $x \in \{0, 1\}$ are obtained by projecting the ancilla onto $\langle x|$ after the full unitary:

$$K_x = \langle x|_{\text{anc}} \cdot (I \otimes R_x(\theta)) \cdot \text{CNOT} \cdot (R_y(\varphi) \otimes I) \cdot |0\rangle_{\text{anc}}$$

We trace through the circuit step by step. Starting from $|\psi\rangle_{\text{mem}} \otimes |0\rangle_{\text{anc}}$:

**Step 1**: Apply $R_y(\varphi) \otimes I$:

$$R_y(\varphi)|\psi\rangle \otimes |0\rangle$$

where $R_y(\varphi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac{\varphi}{2} & -\sin\frac{\varphi}{2} \\ \sin\frac{\varphi}{2} & \cos\frac{\varphi}{2} \end{pmatrix}$.

Writing $R_y(\varphi)|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$:

**Step 2**: Apply CNOT (memory = control, ancilla = target):

$$\alpha|0\rangle|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle|1\rangle$$

**Step 3**: Apply $I \otimes R_x(\theta)$, where $R_x(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac{\theta}{2} & -i\sin\frac{\theta}{2} \\ -i\sin\frac{\theta}{2} & \cos\frac{\theta}{2} \end{pmatrix}$:

Using $R_x(\theta)|0\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle - i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle$ and $R_x(\theta)|1\rangle = -i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle + \cos\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle$:

$$\Big(\alpha\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle - i\beta\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle\Big)|0\rangle + \Big(-i\alpha\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle + \beta\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle\Big)|1\rangle$$

**Step 4**: Project onto ancilla outcome $\langle x|$:

$$K_0|\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\alpha\,|0\rangle - i\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\beta\,|1\rangle$$
$$K_1|\psi\rangle = -i\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\alpha\,|0\rangle + \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\beta\,|1\rangle$$

Since $\alpha = \langle 0|R_y(\varphi)|\psi\rangle$ and $\beta = \langle 1|R_y(\varphi)|\psi\rangle$, the Kraus operators are:

$$\boxed{K_x = D_x \cdot R_y(\varphi)}$$

where $D_0 = \text{diag}\!\left(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2},\; -i\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)$ and $D_1 = \text{diag}\!\left(-i\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2},\; \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)$.

### 3.2 Explicit matrix form

Writing $c_\theta = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}$, $s_\theta = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}$, $c_\varphi = \cos\frac{\varphi}{2}$, $s_\varphi = \sin\frac{\varphi}{2}$:

$$K_0 = \begin{pmatrix} c_\theta\, c_\varphi & -c_\theta\, s_\varphi \\ -is_\theta\, s_\varphi & -is_\theta\, c_\varphi \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -is_\theta\, c_\varphi & is_\theta\, s_\varphi \\ c_\theta\, s_\varphi & c_\theta\, c_\varphi \end{pmatrix}$$

### 3.3 Completeness

The completeness relation $K_0^\dagger K_0 + K_1^\dagger K_1 = I$ follows from the factored form $K_x = D_x R_y(\varphi)$:

$$\sum_{x} K_x^\dagger K_x = R_y(\varphi)^\dagger \left(\sum_{x} D_x^\dagger D_x\right) R_y(\varphi) = R_y(\varphi)^\dagger\, I\, R_y(\varphi) = I$$

since $D_0^\dagger D_0 = \text{diag}(c_\theta^2, s_\theta^2)$ and $D_1^\dagger D_1 = \text{diag}(s_\theta^2, c_\theta^2)$ sum to $I$.

### 3.4 Quantum state update

Given a density matrix $\rho_t$ for the memory qubit, observing token $x_t$ at time $t$:

- **Probability**: $\Pr(x_t = x \mid \rho_t) = \text{tr}(K_x\, \rho_t\, K_x^\dagger)$
- **Bayesian update**: $\rho_{t+1} = \frac{K_x\, \rho_t\, K_x^\dagger}{\text{tr}(K_x\, \rho_t\, K_x^\dagger)}$

The full CPTP channel (marginalizing over measurement outcomes) is:

$$\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}} K_x\, \rho\, K_x^\dagger$$

## 4. GHMM Representation

### 4.1 Extended Bloch vector

Any qubit density matrix can be decomposed in the Pauli basis:

$$\rho = v_0\,\frac{I}{2} + v_1\,\frac{\sigma_x}{2} + v_2\,\frac{\sigma_y}{2} + v_3\,\frac{\sigma_z}{2}$$

where the extended Bloch vector $\mathbf{v} = (v_0, v_1, v_2, v_3)^T$ satisfies $v_0 = \text{tr}(\rho) = 1$ for normalized states. The components $(v_1, v_2, v_3) = (b_x, b_y, b_z)$ form the standard Bloch vector, with $b_x^2 + b_y^2 + b_z^2 \leq 1$.

### 4.2 Transition operators in the Bloch representation

Each subchannel $\mathcal{A}_x(\rho) = K_x \rho K_x^\dagger$ is a linear map on density matrices. In the Bloch basis $\{B_m\}_{m=0}^3 = \{I/2, \sigma_x/2, \sigma_y/2, \sigma_z/2\}$, this map is represented by a $4 \times 4$ real matrix $G^{(x)}$:

$$G^{(x)}_{mn} = 2\,\text{tr}\!\left(B_m\, K_x\, B_n\, K_x^\dagger\right)$$

such that $\mathcal{A}_x(\rho)$ has Bloch vector $G^{(x)} \mathbf{v}$ when $\rho$ has Bloch vector $\mathbf{v}$.

The formula gives $G^{(x)}_{mn}$ as the coefficient of basis element $B_m$ in the output $K_x B_n K_x^\dagger$, using the trace orthogonality $\text{tr}(B_m B_n) = \delta_{mn}/2$.

### 4.3 Row-vector convention

Following the GHMM convention of Riechers, Elliott & Shai (2025), we use row vectors $\langle\!\langle \eta |$ for predictive states and the transition matrices $T^{(x)}$ act from the right:

$$\Pr(x \mid \eta) = \langle\!\langle \eta | \, T^{(x)} \, | 1 \rangle\!\rangle$$

The GHMM transition matrices are the *transposes* of the Bloch-space subchannels:

$$\boxed{T^{(x)} = \left(G^{(x)}\right)^T}$$

with $|1\rangle\!\rangle = \mathbf{e}_0 = (1, 0, 0, 0)^T$ (corresponding to the trace functional) and stationary predictive vector $\langle\!\langle \pi | = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ (corresponding to the fully mixed state $I/2$).

### 4.4 Belief dynamics

Given a predictive vector $\langle\!\langle \eta^{(w)} |$ induced by history $w$, observing token $x$ updates it as:

$$\langle\!\langle \eta^{(wx)} | = \frac{\langle\!\langle \eta^{(w)} | \, T^{(x)}}{\langle\!\langle \eta^{(w)} | \, T^{(x)} \, |1\rangle\!\rangle}$$

The denominator is $\Pr(x \mid w)$, ensuring normalization $\langle\!\langle \eta^{(wx)} | 1 \rangle\!\rangle = 1$.

### 4.5 Net transition operator

The net transition operator $T = T^{(0)} + T^{(1)}$ satisfies:

$$T\,|1\rangle\!\rangle = |1\rangle\!\rangle \qquad \text{and} \qquad \langle\!\langle \pi |\, T = \langle\!\langle \pi |$$

The eigenvalues of the $3 \times 3$ non-trivial block of $T$ (acting on the Bloch vector subspace) determine the contraction rate of the belief dynamics. Their magnitudes are strictly less than 1 for an ergodic process, ensuring convergence of the belief fractal.

## 5. Properties of the Quantum RRXOR

### 5.1 No finite HMM

The Bloch dynamics involve rotations in the $(b_x, b_y, b_z)$ space. When the rotation angle is incommensurate with $\pi$ (which occurs generically for irrational $\varphi/\pi$), the set of reachable belief states is infinite—distinct histories produce distinct predictive vectors, and no finite set of classical states suffices.

Formally: the eigenvalues of the non-trivial block of $T$ include a complex conjugate pair $\lambda, \bar{\lambda}$ with $|\lambda| < 1$ and $\arg(\lambda)/\pi \notin \mathbb{Q}$. The iterative belief updates never return to a previously visited state, so the process requires infinite classical memory but only a single qubit of quantum memory.

### 5.2 Belief geometry is 3-dimensional

Because the Kraus operators $K_x = D_x R_y(\varphi)$ have both real and imaginary matrix elements, the Bloch dynamics generically couple all three components $(b_x, b_y, b_z)$. Starting from the stationary state $\langle\!\langle \pi | = (1, 0, 0, 0)$:

- Token 0 displaces the belief along the $b_z$ axis (from the diagonal $D_0$),
- Subsequent tokens rotate the belief through the $(b_x, b_y, b_z)$ space via the off-diagonal elements introduced by $R_y(\varphi)$,
- The imaginary elements of $K_x$ (the $-i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}$ terms) generate the $b_y$ component.

The result is a 3-dimensional belief geometry—a fractal embedded in the Bloch ball.

### 5.3 Next-token probabilities are 1-dimensional

With only two tokens, the next-token distribution is completely determined by a single scalar:

$$p(0 \mid \eta) = \langle\!\langle \eta | \, T^{(0)} \, |1\rangle\!\rangle$$

since $p(1 \mid \eta) = 1 - p(0 \mid \eta)$. This is a linear functional of the 4-component predictive vector $\eta$, effectively projecting the 3-dimensional belief geometry onto a 1-dimensional line.

### 5.4 Non-invertibility of the belief-to-next-token map

The map from 3D beliefs to 1D next-token probabilities is a projection $\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^1$, which is necessarily **non-invertible**: its kernel is a 2-dimensional subspace. Concretely, the iso-$p(0)$ surfaces are 2-dimensional planes in Bloch space:

$$p(0 \mid b_x, b_y, b_z) = \frac{1}{2} + \alpha_x\, b_x + \alpha_y\, b_y + \alpha_z\, b_z$$

where the coefficients $\alpha_i$ are determined by $T^{(0)}$. All belief states on the same plane share identical next-token probabilities but carry different information about the distant future.

This means: if a neural network's activations linearly encode the full 3D belief geometry (as demonstrated for related processes in Riechers, Elliott & Shai, 2025), the network must be representing something strictly richer than next-token probabilities. No linear probe can recover a 3-dimensional structure from a 1-dimensional encoding.

## 6. GHMM Leakage

### 6.1 General formula

For HMMs, the standard leakage mixes each transition with a uniform distribution over all (token, state) pairs:

$$T^{(x)}_\varepsilon = (1 - \varepsilon)\, T^{(x)} + \frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{X}| \cdot |\mathcal{S}|}\, \mathbf{J}$$

The GHMM analog replaces "uniform over states" (which has no meaning for a GHMM's non-probabilistic latent vectors) with "reset to stationary":

$$\boxed{T^{(x)}_\varepsilon = (1 - \varepsilon)\, T^{(x)} + \frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{X}|}\, |1\rangle\!\rangle \langle\!\langle \pi |}$$

### 6.2 Properties preserved under leakage

This leakage preserves the GHMM structure:

- **Right eigenvector**: $T_\varepsilon |1\rangle\!\rangle = (1-\varepsilon)\,T|1\rangle\!\rangle + \varepsilon\,|1\rangle\!\rangle = |1\rangle\!\rangle$ ✓
- **Left eigenvector**: $\langle\!\langle \pi | T_\varepsilon = (1-\varepsilon)\,\langle\!\langle \pi | + \varepsilon\,\langle\!\langle \pi | = \langle\!\langle \pi |$ ✓
- **Eigenvalue scaling**: Non-trivial eigenvalues become $(1-\varepsilon)\lambda$, introducing contraction.

### 6.3 Physical interpretation

The leakage adds, with probability $\varepsilon$, a "reset" step: forget the current memory state and return to the stationary (fully mixed) state, while emitting each token with its stationary probability. This is the quantum analog of classical dephasing—it gradually destroys quantum coherence in the memory.

For the Quantum RRXOR, where $T$ already has eigenvalues with $|\lambda| < 1$, leakage simply accelerates the contraction, making the self-similar fractal structure of the belief geometry more pronounced at fewer levels of depth.

## 7. Limiting Cases

### 7.1 Classical limit: $\theta \to \pi$

As $\theta \to \pi$, $\cos\frac{\theta}{2} \to 0$ and $\sin\frac{\theta}{2} \to 1$:

$$K_0 \to \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -is_\varphi & -ic_\varphi \end{pmatrix} = -i\,|1\rangle\langle 1|\,R_y(\varphi), \qquad K_1 \to \begin{pmatrix} -ic_\varphi & is_\varphi \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -i\,|0\rangle\langle 0|\,R_y(\varphi)$$

These are rank-1: token 0 projects the memory onto $|1\rangle$ and token 1 projects onto $|0\rangle$ (after rotation). This is a complete measurement—the memory fully decoheres, and the process becomes classical.

### 7.2 Uninformative limit: $\theta \to 0$

As $\theta \to 0$, $K_0 \to R_y(\varphi)$ and $K_1 \to 0$. Token 0 occurs with probability 1 from every state, and token 1 never occurs. The process degenerates—every observation is the same.

### 7.3 No rotation: $\varphi = 0$

When $\varphi = 0$, $R_y(0) = I$, so $K_x = D_x$—diagonal matrices. The Bloch dynamics act only on $b_z$, and the belief geometry is 1-dimensional. The process has a finite (2-state) HMM.

## 8. Connection to Prior Work

### 8.1 Riechers & Crutchfield (2021)

The Quantum RRXOR construction follows directly from the quantum circuit framework for stochastic processes developed in "Fraudulent White Noise" (Phys. Rev. Research 3, 013170). Figure 8 of that paper shows how classical processes (including RRXOR-like structures) can be represented as quantum circuits with intermediate measurements. The Quantum RRXOR generalizes panel (c) of that figure—the "correlated Bell process"—by keeping the memory qubit fully coherent.

### 8.2 Riechers, Elliott & Shai (2025)

The Quantum RRXOR serves as a natural test case for the results of "Neural networks leverage nominally quantum and post-quantum representations" (arXiv:2507.07432). With $|\mathcal{X}| = 2$ and $\dim(\text{beliefs}) = 3$, it provides the cleanest possible demonstration that neural networks trained on this process must represent beliefs (not just next-token probabilities), since no linear probe can recover a 3D structure from a 1D signal.

### 8.3 Monràs & Winter (2016); Fanizza, Lumbreras & Winter (2024)

The Quantum RRXOR falls in the class $\mathcal{Q}$ of processes simulable by finite-dimensional quantum memory (specifically, a single qubit). For generic parameters, it is not in the class $\mathcal{C}$ of processes with finite classical HMMs. The GHMM representation has $d^2 = 4$ dimensions, with 3 effective dimensions after normalization.

$$\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$R_x(\theta) = e^{-i\theta\sigma_x/2} = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,I - i\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac{\theta}{2} & -i\sin\frac{\theta}{2} \\ -i\sin\frac{\theta}{2} & \cos\frac{\theta}{2} \end{pmatrix}$$

$$R_y(\varphi) = e^{-i\varphi\sigma_y/2} = \cos\tfrac{\varphi}{2}\,I - i\sin\tfrac{\varphi}{2}\,\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac{\varphi}{2} & -\sin\frac{\varphi}{2} \\ \sin\frac{\varphi}{2} & \cos\frac{\varphi}{2} \end{pmatrix}$$

## Appendix B: GHMM Convention Correspondence

The subchannel $\mathcal{A}_x(\rho) = K_x \rho K_x^\dagger$ maps column Bloch vectors: $\mathbf{v}' = G^{(x)} \mathbf{v}$.

The GHMM convention uses row predictive vectors: $\langle\!\langle \eta' | = \langle\!\langle \eta | \, T^{(x)}$.

These are related by transposition:

$$T^{(x)}_{ij} = G^{(x)}_{ji} = 2\,\text{tr}\!\left(B_j\, K_x\, B_i\, K_x^\dagger\right)$$

Equivalently: $\langle\!\langle \eta | \, T^{(x)} = \left(G^{(x)} \cdot \eta^T\right)^T$, so the row-vector update $\eta \cdot T$ gives the same result as the column-vector update $G \cdot v$ when $\eta = v^T$.
